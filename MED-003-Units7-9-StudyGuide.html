Definition

Energy planning is a systematic process of analysing current energy use, forecasting future demand, assessing available resources, and formulating strategies to meet those needs reliably, affordably and sustainably. It applies across scales — household, city, state, national, and global.

The 6-Step Planning Cycle
Energy Planning Cycle — iterative, not one-time
📌 Aims of Energy Planning
  • Ensure reliable supply to all sectors — households, industry, agriculture, services
  • Minimise environmental impact — reduce pollution, shift to clean sources
  • Promote efficiency — do more with less energy everywhere
  • Achieve energy security — reduce vulnerability to import disruptions or price shocks
  • Ensure equitable access — rural electrification, affordable tariffs for the poor
  • Support economic growth — adequate energy for industry and job creation
  • Meet climate commitments — align with NDCs and Paris Agreement
✅ Benefits of Good Energy Planning
  • Avoids overcapacity and shortage — no expensive stranded assets, no blackouts
  • Lower energy costs — efficient planning reduces total system cost for consumers
  • Attracts private investment — clear long-term plan gives investors confidence
  • Reduces emissions — planned renewable rollout displaces coal systematically
  • Improves energy security — diverse sources reduce import dependence
  • Universal access — planned rural electrification reaches the poorest last-mile
Drawbacks of Faulty Planning (Terminal Q5)
  • Power shortages and blackouts — under-estimation of future demand
  • Stranded assets — over-built capacity that cannot be economically used (e.g. thermal plants becoming unviable as solar prices fall)
  • Environmental damage — poorly sited projects destroy forests, rivers, communities
  • High consumer prices — inefficient planning passes costs to end users
  • Rural exclusion — remote areas remain without reliable supply for decades
  • Import dependency — failure to develop domestic resources leaves the country exposed to global price shocks
Relevance for Developing Countries (Terminal Q1)

Energy planning is MORE critical in developing nations than in developed ones because: (a) capital is scarce — wrong investments are catastrophic; (b) energy demand is growing fastest; (c) millions lack basic access — planning must prioritise equity; (d) weak institutional capacity means mistakes persist longer; (e) climate vulnerability is highest — transition to clean energy is both urgent and essential for survival.

What is Energy Infrastructure Planning?

It means designing cities and buildings from the start so they use as little energy as possible — through smart location of buildings, transport links, green spaces, and utility networks. Good planning reduces energy demand permanently; bad planning locks in waste for 50–100 years.

7 Guidelines for Energy-Efficient Land Use Planning
  1. Mixed-use zoning — put homes, shops, offices and services in the same neighbourhood so people walk or cycle instead of driving. Less transport energy = less fuel burned.
  2. Compact urban form — dense, connected city blocks rather than sprawling suburbs. Shorter distances → less transport energy; shared walls → less heating/cooling energy.
  3. Transit-Oriented Development (TOD) — cluster high-density housing and commercial uses around metro stations and bus hubs. People naturally use public transport → massive transport energy savings.
  4. District heating and cooling networks — a centralised CHP (Combined Heat and Power) plant supplies waste heat through insulated pipes to multiple buildings. Far more efficient than every building heating itself separately.
  5. Solar-ready site orientation — orient buildings so roofs face south (in India) to maximise solar panel output. Passive solar design — large south-facing windows allow winter sun to heat rooms naturally.
  6. Building Energy Codes (ECBC) — make energy efficiency standards mandatory for all new commercial buildings above a certain size. India's ECBC (Energy Conservation Building Code) is now enforceable.
  7. Green corridors and urban forests — trees and parks reduce the urban heat island effect → less air conditioning needed across the entire city.
Benefits of Urban Energy Planning (Dec-24 Q6c)
  • Reduces peak electricity demand → smaller, cheaper power infrastructure needed
  • Lower energy bills for residents and businesses
  • Cleaner air from less vehicle and building emissions
  • More resilient to energy price shocks — efficient city uses less fuel
  • Supports climate commitments — planned cities are the fastest route to net-zero
India examples: Smart Cities Mission (100 cities), Delhi Metro (40% energy from solar), Amaravati planned city, GIFT City (Gujarat) — all use energy planning principles.

🧊 REFRIGERATOR — Energy Saving Tips
  1. Buy 5-star BEE rated model — uses 30–40% less electricity than 2-star
  2. Keep it away from heat sources — ovens, direct sunlight force compressor to work harder
  3. Set temperature correctly — 3–5°C for fridge, -18°C for freezer. Every degree colder = 5% more energy
  4. Keep it full — full fridge retains cold better when door opens; use water bottles to fill gaps
  5. Check door seal regularly — a leaking seal makes the compressor run continuously
  6. Let hot food cool before putting in — hot food forces the motor to work extra
  7. Clean condenser coils — dusty coils reduce efficiency by 25%
  8. Defrost regularly — ice build-up reduces efficiency significantly
🫧 WASHING MACHINE — Energy Saving Tips
  1. Always use full loads — half-load uses almost the same energy as full load; wait and combine
  2. Wash in cold water — 90% of washing machine energy goes to heating water; cold wash works fine for most laundry
  3. Choose front-loading machines — use 60% less water and 40% less energy than top-loaders
  4. Use eco/quick mode — shorter cycles for lightly soiled clothes
  5. Air dry instead of tumble drying — tumble dryer uses as much electricity as the washing cycle
  6. Wash at off-peak hours — time-of-use tariffs make electricity cheaper at night
  7. Buy 5-star BEE rated model — significant savings over 10-year life of machine

What is a Home Energy Audit?

A home energy audit is a systematic inspection of a house to identify where energy is being wasted and what measures will be most cost-effective to fix it. It is like a health check-up for your house's energy use. It looks at every system — building envelope, heating/cooling, lighting, appliances, and hot water — and produces a prioritised action list with estimated savings and payback periods.

8 Aspects of Conservation Checked in a Home Audit
  1. Building envelope — roof insulation, wall insulation, floor insulation. Is heat escaping in winter? Is it entering in summer? Thermal imaging cameras reveal hot spots.
  2. Windows and doors — single vs double glazing, gaps in door frames, quality of seals. Gaps cause "invisible" energy losses of 15–25%.
  3. Lighting — are all bulbs LED? Are lights left on in empty rooms? Motion sensors and timers can cut lighting energy 40–60%.
  4. Appliances — age and star rating of fridge, AC, washing machine, water heater, TV. Replacing a 10-year-old 2-star fridge with a 5-star model pays back within 3 years.
  5. Heating and cooling (HVAC) — AC efficiency rating (ISEER), thermostat settings, duct leaks, filter cleanliness. AC is typically 40–50% of the electricity bill.
  6. Water heating — electric geyser vs solar water heater. Solar pays back in 3–5 years in most Indian cities.
  7. Standby loads — TVs, chargers, set-top boxes left on standby consume 10% of home energy silently. A power strip with a master switch eliminates this.
  8. Renewable energy potential — roof area and orientation for solar panels, estimate of generation vs consumption.

1 paper Short note: Demand-Side Management (DSM) ▶
Dec-21 Q6bSAQ 2
Definition

DSM is the planning, implementation and monitoring of activities by utilities and governments to influence WHEN and HOW MUCH electricity customers use. The goal is to flatten the demand curve — reduce peaks and fill valleys — so less generation capacity is needed overall.

Why DSM?

The electricity grid must be built for PEAK demand — but peak lasts only a few hours per day. A power plant sitting idle 20 hours/day to serve 4 hours of peak is hugely wasteful. DSM shifts demand away from peaks → smaller grid needed → lower investment → cheaper electricity.

5 Key DSM Strategies
  1. Time-of-Use (TOU) tariffs — electricity is cheaper at night and expensive in peak hours. Consumers shift washing machines, EV charging, and industrial operations to off-peak times.
  2. Energy efficiency standards — mandatory minimum efficiency for appliances, buildings, and motors removes the least efficient products from the market permanently.
  3. Smart meters — real-time consumption data helps consumers see and respond to their energy use. Utilities can remotely manage loads during grid emergencies.
  4. Load shedding programmes — large industrial consumers voluntarily reduce load during peaks in exchange for lower tariffs (interruptible supply contracts).
  5. Energy audit programmes — professional audits identify waste in homes and commercial buildings; result in efficiency investments that permanently reduce demand.
Key point: DSM is the cheapest MW — "negawatt" (a megawatt of demand that never needs to be generated) is always cheaper than building a new power plant. India's BEE runs the PAT scheme and UJALA as the country's main DSM programmes.

Multi-Use Building Concept

A multi-use (mixed-use) building combines offices, residences, retail and services in one structure. Energy benefit: each use has a different peak demand time — offices peak at 9–5, residences at 6–11pm, restaurants at 12–2pm and 7–10pm. Combined, the load curve is flatter throughout the day — so the building needs smaller HVAC equipment and draws less peak power from the grid. Shared infrastructure (elevators, parking, HVAC, solar panels) also reduces energy per square metre compared to separate buildings.

Infrastructure Efficiency Strategies (Jun-20 Q2a)
  1. Power supply — reduce T&D (transmission and distribution) losses; India average is 20%, world best practice is 5–6%. Smart grid technology monitors losses in real time.
  2. Water supply — energy-efficient pumps, leak detection (30–40% of treated water is lost to leaks in India), gravity flow designs to reduce pumping energy. Water pumping is often the biggest electricity cost for municipalities.
  3. Wastewater — biogas from sewage treatment plants (sewage sludge ferments to methane); India's Namami Gange programme includes biogas recovery from sewage.
  4. Recycled water — treated effluent used for industrial cooling towers reduces freshwater demand AND pumping energy.
  5. Solid waste — waste-to-energy plants convert municipal solid waste (MSW) to electricity; India generates 1.5 lakh tonnes MSW/day — largely wasted potential.
  6. Smart grid — real-time monitoring, automated fault detection, and integration of distributed solar generation all reduce system losses and improve resilience.

Unresolved Issues in India's Energy Sector
  1. DISCOM financial stress — state distribution companies (DISCOMs) owe ₹6+ lakh crore in accumulated losses. They cannot invest in grid upgrades or pay generators on time. Root cause: politically-motivated free/cheap electricity to agriculture.
  2. High T&D losses (~20%) — electricity theft, old equipment and poor metering mean 20% of generated electricity is never billed. World best practice is 5–6%.
  3. Unreliable rural supply — villages are "electrified" on paper but get 4–8 hours of supply daily. Electrification ≠ reliable electricity.
  4. Coal logistics bottlenecks — Coal India supply disruptions, inadequate rail capacity to move coal from mines to power plants → plants run at low capacity factors.
  5. Grid integration of renewables — solar and wind are variable. Old grid infrastructure cannot absorb large shares of renewables without storage and grid flexibility upgrades.
  6. Land acquisition delays — solar parks, wind farms, and transmission lines all face delays due to land rights, forest clearance, and community opposition.
  7. Cooking fuel transition — 60 million+ households received Ujjwala LPG connections but cannot afford refills; revert to firewood. Last-mile affordability remains unsolved.

Definition

Energy economics is the application of economic principles to the production, distribution, consumption and regulation of energy. It asks: How much energy will be demanded at what price? What is the most efficient energy supply mix? How do we price pollution? How do energy subsidies distort markets? It bridges engineering (how energy is produced) with economics (who pays, how much, and what are the incentives).

Key Concerns / Factors in Energy Economics
  1. Energy demand and its response to price — how much does demand change when price rises? (see price elasticity below)
  2. Energy supply costs — capital cost, operating cost, fuel cost, and externalities (pollution costs) of each energy source
  3. Market structure — is the energy sector a natural monopoly (electricity distribution) or competitive (electricity generation)? Monopolies need regulation.
  4. Subsidies and their distortions — energy subsidies keep prices below cost, encouraging wasteful consumption and discouraging investment in efficiency
  5. Environmental externalities — costs of pollution (health damage, climate change) are not included in market prices; economic instruments (carbon tax, cap-and-trade) are needed to correct this
  6. Energy security economics — the cost of import dependence, price volatility risk, and value of domestic energy sources for national security
  7. Investment economics — long-lived energy infrastructure (power plants, pipelines, grids) needs long-term price certainty; uncertainty kills investment
Price Elasticity and Inelasticity of Energy Demand (Dec-22 Q3b)

Price elasticity of demand = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price). If a 10% price rise causes a 5% drop in consumption, elasticity = -0.5 (inelastic).

📌 Price Inelastic (short run)
  • If electricity prices rise 20%, people still need to cook, heat, run factories
  • Elasticity typically -0.1 to -0.4 in short run
  • Cannot switch appliances overnight — locked into existing equipment
  • Energy is a necessity — no substitutes in the short run
  • Implication: carbon taxes must be sustained long-term to change behaviour
📌 More Elastic (long run)
  • Over 5–10 years, people buy efficient appliances, insulate homes, switch fuels
  • Elasticity typically -0.5 to -1.0 in long run
  • Industries invest in efficient equipment; switch from oil to gas
  • High long-run price signals → innovation in clean technology
  • Oil price spike 1973 → triggered 30 years of energy efficiency innovation in OECD
💡 Key exam point: Energy demand is a derived demand — people don't want energy itself, they want warm rooms, cooked food, light, and mobility. This is the basis of the "end-use energy services approach" — focus on providing services efficiently, not on supplying maximum energy quantity.

Definition and Scope

Environmental economics applies economic tools to understand and solve environmental problems. It studies how economic activity affects the environment and how environmental policies affect the economy. Its central insight: markets fail on environmental goods — clean air and a stable climate are not bought and sold in markets, so they get over-used and degraded (the "tragedy of the commons").

Scope includes: valuing environmental damage, designing carbon markets, analysing cost-effectiveness of pollution controls, measuring the economics of biodiversity loss, and evaluating environmental policies.

Two Methods of Environmental Economics (Dec-21 Q2b)
① Command and Control (CAC)
  • Government sets mandatory standards — emission limits, fuel standards, technology requirements
  • Polluters MUST comply or face fines/shutdown
  • Example: India's emission norms for coal plants (SO₂, NOₓ, particulate limits); vehicle emission standards (BS6)
  • Advantage: certainty — the standard is achieved if enforced
  • Disadvantage: one-size-fits-all; no incentive to do better than the standard; expensive to monitor and enforce
② Market-Based Instruments (MBI)
  • Use price signals to change behaviour — make polluting expensive, clean cheap
  • Carbon tax: polluters pay per tonne of CO₂ → incentive to reduce emissions
  • Cap and Trade: total pollution capped; permits traded → cheapest reductions happen first (EU Emissions Trading System, ETS)
  • Advantage: cost-effective — reductions happen where they are cheapest; incentive to innovate beyond standard
  • Disadvantage: uncertain quantity outcome; politically difficult; can be gamed
Justifying the Statement (Jun-19 Q6b)

"Environmental economics undertakes theoretical or empirical studies of the economic effects of national or local environmental policies around the world." This is justified because:

  • Theoretical studies — develop models of how carbon taxes will shift energy mix, how cap-and-trade markets find equilibrium prices, how subsidy removal affects consumption
  • Empirical studies — measure actual outcomes: did Europe's ETS reduce emissions? Did India's PAT scheme cut industrial energy intensity? By how much? At what cost?
  • Policy evaluation — compare effectiveness and cost of different instruments (tax vs standard vs subsidy vs trading) to advise governments on what works best in specific contexts
  • Cross-country comparisons — what policy mix achieved the best results in Germany, Denmark, China, India? Generalise lessons for other nations
  • Without this analysis, energy and climate policy would be guesswork — environmental economics provides the evidence base for smart decisions
1 paper — 10 marks Economics of demand and supply of energy OR Problem of energy in eliminating poverty without polluting ▶
Dec-20 Q4 — 10M Either/Or
Option A: Economics of Demand and Supply of Energy

Demand side: Energy demand is derived demand — people want services (warmth, light, mobility), not energy itself. Demand is price-inelastic in short run (people can't switch technologies quickly) but more elastic long-term. Demand drivers: income, population, industrialisation, urbanisation, technology. DSM (Demand Side Management) can shift demand away from expensive peaks.

Supply side: Energy supply involves capital-intensive, long-lived assets. Supply economics are determined by: capital cost (CAPEX), operating cost (OPEX), fuel cost, and externalities. The LCOE (Levelised Cost of Energy) allows fair comparison across technologies — solar LCOE in India is now ~₹2–3/kWh, cheaper than new coal. Supply responds slowly to price signals due to long construction lead times.

Market equilibrium: In theory, price equates demand and supply. But energy markets fail because: monopoly power in transmission/distribution; environmental externalities not priced; information asymmetries; long-term investment horizon vs short-term markets. Hence, energy markets always need regulation and policy intervention.

Option B: Problem of Energy in Eliminating Poverty Without Polluting

The central dilemma: 700+ million people globally lack reliable electricity; 2.4 billion use dirty biomass for cooking. Lifting them out of poverty requires energy. But the conventional path — coal-based industrialisation — will make climate change catastrophic for the very same poor people, who are most vulnerable to floods, droughts and heat waves.

The poverty-pollution-energy nexus:

  • Energy poverty causes local pollution — biomass cooking kills 4 million people/year from indoor air pollution, mostly poor women and children in developing nations
  • Development needs energy — no hospital can function without electricity; no cold chain for vaccines; no irrigation without pumps; no factory without power
  • Clean energy is the solution, not the obstacle — solar home systems, biogas, and mini-grids can deliver clean energy to the rural poor often cheaper than grid extension
  • Leapfrogging — developing nations can skip the dirty coal phase entirely, as mobile phones skipped landlines. Bangladesh built solar home systems for 6 million households without ever having grid electricity.
  • Finance is the missing link — clean energy technology exists; the gap is affordable upfront capital. Climate finance ($100 billion/year promised but not delivered by North) and green bonds can close this gap.
Key argument: The choice is NOT "poverty OR clean environment." Distributed renewable energy can deliver both — clean, affordable, reliable energy to the poor AND meet climate targets. The Sustainable Development Goal 7 (SDG7) — Affordable and Clean Energy for All — makes this the explicit global commitment.
Terminal Q3 Demand-side, end-use oriented, energy-services approach in energy economics ▶
Terminal Q3SAQ 4
What is the Energy Services Approach?

Traditional energy planning asked: "How much energy will we need?" The end-use energy services approach asks instead: "What services do people actually need, and what is the least-energy way to deliver them?"

People don't want kilowatt-hours — they want:

  • Warmth — can be provided by less heating energy if the house is well-insulated
  • Light — 10W LED gives same light as 100W incandescent bulb
  • Cooked food — pressure cooker uses 3× less energy than open pot
  • Cold storage — 5-star refrigerator uses 40% less energy for same cooling
  • Mobility — metro system moves 40,000 people/hr on electricity; same as 20,000 cars but 1/10th the energy

Why This Approach Matters
  • Shifts focus from supply maximisation to service delivery — government doesn't need to build more plants if demand can be met with efficiency
  • Identifies the cheapest route to the same outcome — insulating a building is always cheaper than generating extra heating electricity
  • Breaks the assumed link between GDP growth and energy consumption growth — a service economy can grow rapidly with modest energy growth if efficiency improves fast enough
  • Supports the "negawatt" concept — a megawatt of demand that is permanently reduced by efficiency is worth more than a megawatt of new supply, because it keeps reducing costs forever
💡 Example: India's UJALA programme replaced 360 million incandescent bulbs with LEDs. Same light service, 80% less electricity. This "created" ~20,000 MW of virtual generation capacity — equivalent to 20 large power plants — without building a single generator.
Terminal Q4 Imperatives of energy economics for sustainable development ▶
Terminal Q4SAQ 3 — indirect empirical methodologies
4 Economic Imperatives for Sustainable Energy Development
  1. Price environmental externalities — fossil fuels must reflect their true cost including health damage, climate damage and ecosystem destruction. IMF estimates global fossil fuel subsidies (including unpriced externalities) at $7 trillion/year. Until pollution is priced, markets will always choose the dirtiest cheapest option. Carbon tax or cap-and-trade corrects this market failure.
  2. Invest in long-term infrastructure — sustainable energy systems require patient capital. Solar parks last 25 years, grids 40 years, nuclear plants 50 years. Short-term financial thinking (quarterly profits) is incompatible with sustainable energy investment. Development banks, sovereign wealth funds, and green bonds provide the long-term capital that energy sustainability requires.
  3. Energy access as economic development — energy economics for sustainable development must treat energy access for the poor not as charity but as productive investment. Every Rs.1 spent on rural electrification generates Rs.4–6 in economic activity through agriculture, small businesses, healthcare and education. The economic case for universal energy access is overwhelming.
  4. Decouple economic growth from energy consumption — sustainable development requires growing GDP without proportionally growing energy use. This is achieved through efficiency (energy intensity declining), structural shift to services (less energy-intensive than manufacturing), and clean technology. Germany's GDP grew 25% while energy consumption fell 15% between 2000–2020 — proof that decoupling is achievable.
Indirect Empirical Methodologies (SAQ 3)

When environmental damage cannot be directly observed or priced, economists use indirect methods to estimate its value:

  • Hedonic pricing — house prices near a polluted power plant are lower; the price difference reveals the economic cost people assign to living near pollution
  • Travel cost method — how much do people spend travelling to clean natural areas? Reveals their willingness to pay for clean environment
  • Contingent valuation — survey people: "How much would you pay to prevent this pollution?" Measures stated preferences even for non-market goods
  • Damage cost approach — calculate actual health costs, crop losses, and infrastructure damage caused by specific pollution sources (coal plant SO₂ → hospital admissions → cost per tonne SO₂)
